Renewable Energy Technologies

Renewable energy comes from naturally replenished sources such as sunlight, wind, rain,
tides, waves, and geothermal heat. Unlike fossil fuels, renewables produce little to no
greenhouse gas emissions during operation.

Solar Energy
Photovoltaic (PV) cells convert sunlight directly into electricity using the photoelectric
effect. Solar panel costs have fallen by more than 90% since 2010, making solar the cheapest
source of electricity in history in many regions. Concentrated solar power (CSP) uses mirrors
to focus sunlight and generate steam that drives turbines.

Wind Energy
Wind turbines convert kinetic energy from wind into electricity. Onshore wind is one of the
lowest-cost electricity sources available. Offshore wind farms benefit from stronger, more
consistent winds and are expanding rapidly in Europe, Asia, and North America.

Energy Storage
Intermittency — the fact that sun and wind are not always available — is the central challenge
for renewable energy. Lithium-ion batteries are the dominant storage technology today.
Grid-scale battery storage, pumped hydro, and emerging technologies like hydrogen fuel cells
and flow batteries help balance supply and demand.

Hydropower
Hydropower converts the energy of flowing water into electricity. It is the largest source of
renewable electricity globally, accounting for about 16% of world electricity production.
Large dams can have significant ecological impacts; run-of-river projects are a lower-impact
alternative.

Geothermal Energy
Geothermal power plants tap heat from beneath the Earth's surface. Enhanced geothermal systems
(EGS) are expanding the geographic range of this technology beyond volcanic regions.

The transition to renewable energy requires not just new generation capacity but also upgrades
to electricity grids, demand-side management, and policy frameworks that support investment
and fair market access.
